Foundational Journal of Forensic Sciences paper proposing a five-layer ontology that organises digital forensics into disciplines and subdisciplines for shared vocabulary, curriculum, certification, and tool-scoping use—not for case-level evidence exchange.

## Citation
Karie, N. M., & Venter, H. S. (2014). Toward a general ontology for digital forensic disciplines. *Journal of Forensic Sciences*, 59(5), 1231–1241. https://doi.org/10.1111/1556-4029.12511

## Scope note
- This is primarily a **domain-taxonomy / disciplines ontology** (what DF specialisations exist and how they nest), not a UCO/CASE-style **investigation-evidence exchange ontology**.
- Object and subobject lists are explicitly non-exhaustive examples for study; authors invite extension as technology evolves.
- Authors note some listed items may not be mutually exclusive across disciplines—relationship formalisation is flagged as future work.
- Useful for curriculum, CyBOK-adjacent scoping, and AGAFA domain-boundary discussions; weaker as an operational evidence graph for neuro-symbolic validation.

## Key points
- Argues DF ontology research was sparse circa 2012–2014 relative to other domains, and proposes a shared conceptualisation to support reuse, common semantics, and cooperation among practitioners, LE, and educators.
- Five-layer structure (left-to-right): **digital forensics** → **disciplines** → **subdisciplines** → **objects** → **subobjects**.
- Six top-level disciplines: **computer**, **software**, **database**, **multimedia**, **device**, and **network** forensics.
- Illustrative breakdowns include: computer (server/laptop/desktop); software (OS, application, forensic-tool analysis; open-source vs proprietary); database (content/metadata); multimedia (image/video/audio authenticity); device (peripheral, network-enabled, storage, large-scale, small-scale, obscure); network (cloud, telecom, internet, wireless).
- Emphasises “potential evidence” language until reporting/court competence criteria are met, and stresses chain-of-custody / scientific-validity framing for computer forensics.
- Positions intended uses as professional specialisation, certification pathways, DF tool coverage scoping, and higher-education curriculum/module design (including prerequisites to reduce conceptual redundancy).
- Related prior work cited includes Brinson et al. cyber-forensics ontology, SSDD ontology, digital-evidence ontology proposals, and Hoss & Carver “weaving ontologies” for analysis automation—distinguished here by focus on **discipline organisation** rather than single-device or evidence-object modelling alone.
